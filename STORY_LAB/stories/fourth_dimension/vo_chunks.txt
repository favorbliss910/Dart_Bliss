normal | Then, what something else could do to you. And finally, why we are stuck here at all. Some of it is not entirely comfortable.

turn | Part one. A world without up.

normal | In eighteen eighty-four, an English headmaster named Edwin Abbott published a strange little book. It described a world that was completely flat.
normal | Like a sheet of paper, stretching on forever. He called it Flatland.

normal | Let us meet one of its residents. A small square. We will call him Sam.

normal | Sam can move forward and back. He can move left and right. But up? Sam has no word for it. His entire universe has no up.

normal | And Sam cannot look down on his world, the way we look down on a drawing. His eyes sit inside it. He only sees edges.
normal | Every object around him appears as a single line. Brighter here. Darker there.

normal | Sam's home is a shape, surrounded by walls. And those walls are simply lines. When he locks his door, he closes a gap in that line.
normal | And in his world, that is enough. Nothing can get in.

normal | Except, of course, you.

normal | You could reach down from above, into the middle of his locked home. To Sam, a hand would appear from nowhere. Inside a sealed room. And then, vanish.

normal | Hold on to that image. Because in this story, you are Sam. And somewhere, there may be something looking down on you.

turn | Part two. The eye.

normal | If Sam sees only lines, what do we see?

normal | The answer is surprising. We believe we see the world in three dimensions. We do not.

normal | At the back of your eye is the retina. A thin sheet of light-sensitive cells. Essentially, a two-dimensional screen. Light lands on it and paints a flat picture.
normal | Your brain then rebuilds depth, using two eyes, shadows, and a lifetime of experience.

normal | So you live in three dimensions, but you see in two. Sam lives in two dimensions, but sees in one.

normal | Take that pattern one step further. A creature living in four dimensions would have a retina that is not a sheet, but a solid.
normal | A block of light-sensitive cells, filled all the way through.

normal | And that changes everything.

normal | A flat image of a box shows you only its surface. But a solid image of a box contains every point within it.
normal | The outside, and the inside, together.

normal | So with four-dimensional sight, a closed box would simply show you what it holds. Not like an x-ray, faint and transparent.
normal | More like the way you see Sam's home from above. Every room. Every wall. Every interior. All at once, in full detail.

normal | You could look at another person and see their skin, their muscle, their bones, and their beating heart. All at the same time. From every side.

normal | To eyes like that, nothing could be hidden. Not in a pocket. Not in a vault. Not inside a body.

turn | Part three. Visitors.

normal | So what would a four-dimensional object look like, if it passed through our world?

normal | Let us go back to Sam. Imagine lowering a ball through his flat universe.

normal | Sam would not see a ball. He would see a single point, appearing out of nothing. The point would grow into a circle. The circle would widen.
normal | Then shrink. Then disappear entirely.

normal | Sam would call it a miracle. You would call it a ball, passing through.

normal | Now, take that one level higher.

normal | Mathematicians describe a four-dimensional ball, called a hypersphere. If one passed through our world, you would see a tiny point appear in empty air.
normal | It would swell into a sphere. Grow larger. Then smaller. And then, it would be gone.

normal | No door. No direction of arrival. Just presence, and then absence.

normal | The four-dimensional cube, called a tesseract, is stranger still. We draw it as a cube inside a cube, folding through itself as it turns.
normal | But that is only its shadow. To a four-dimensional eye, it is perfectly simple.

normal | The strangeness is ours. Not the shape's.

turn | Part four. A day in four dimensions.

normal | Now, let us try something.

normal | Imagine you wake up tomorrow, and something has changed. You can move in the fourth direction.

normal | It even has a name. More than a century ago, the mathematician Charles Hinton gave the two ends of this direction their own words. Ana, and kata.
normal | Like up, and down. Just pointed somewhere else.

normal | So, you try it. You step ana.

normal | And your house opens like a flower.

normal | You are not above it. You are not beside it. You are outside of it, in a way you have never been outside of anything. Every room.
normal | Every drawer. The inside of every wall. All in plain sight.

normal | In the kitchen, you notice your keys. They are sitting on the counter, inside a house you locked last night. You do not need them.
normal | You step kata, and you are standing beside them.

normal | The door never opened. The lock never turned. For you, walls are now just suggestions.

normal | You head outside. You bend down to tie your shoes. You pull the laces tight. And the knot slides apart in your hands.

normal | You try again. It happens again.

normal | A knot only holds because a rope cannot pass through itself. But now, every rope near you has an extra direction to slip into. And it takes it.
normal | In four dimensions, nothing stays tied.

normal | Then, you remember Sam.

normal | You lean over his flat world, the way you always could. But now, you do something you never could before.

normal | Beside Sam is a footprint. A left footprint. Sam could slide it anywhere, and spin it any way he liked.
normal | But he could never turn it into a right footprint. Nothing in his flat world could.

normal | You lift it out of Flatland. You flip it over. You lay it back down.

normal | Sam stares at it. It is now a right footprint. To him, the impossible has just happened, quietly, in front of his eyes.

normal | Walking through walls. Untying every knot. Turning anything into its reflection.

normal | If you could keep only one of these abilities, which would you choose? Tell me in the comments. And think about it carefully.

turn | Part six. Nothing stays tied.

normal | So if the fourth dimension is so powerful, why don't we live there?

normal | To find out, let us run an experiment. We take our solar system, exactly as it is, and give it one extra direction.

normal | At first, nothing seems wrong. The Sun still shines. The Earth still circles it.

normal | Then, a tiny nudge. A slight tug from Jupiter. The kind of thing that happens all the time.

normal | In our universe, that barely matters. The Earth wobbles, and settles into a slightly different path. Our orbits are forgiving.

normal | But with four dimensions, gravity spreads out into more space, and it fades far faster with distance.
normal | In nineteen seventeen, the physicist Paul Ehrenfest worked out what that means. There are no forgiving orbits anymore.

normal | So the nudge grows. The Earth drifts a little closer to the Sun. Gravity grips a little harder. It drifts closer still.
normal | Until the Earth spirals down, into the fire.

normal | Or, nudged the other way, it slips loose, and sails off into the dark. Forever.

normal | Now, zoom in. All the way down, to a single atom.

normal | An electron around a nucleus faces the same problem. In four dimensions, it has no stable place to settle. Atoms as we know them could not hold together.
normal | No atoms, no chemistry. No chemistry, no life.

normal | Remember your shoelaces? In four dimensions, nothing stays tied. Not knots. Not planets. Not atoms.

normal | So what about fewer dimensions? What about Sam?

normal | Sam, it turns out, has a problem of his own. Think about what it means to eat.
normal | Food goes in one end of a tube, and out the other. But draw a tube straight through Sam's flat body, and you have cut him in two.
normal | As Stephen Hawking once pointed out, a creature in two dimensions would be split apart by its own digestive tract.

normal | Sam, sadly, could never have eaten lunch.

normal | Too few dimensions, and a body falls apart. Too many, and nothing holds together at all.

normal | Three is the narrow space in between. Just enough room for life to be built. Not so much that it all comes undone.

normal | So three dimensions are not a limitation. They may be the very thing that allows us to exist.

normal | And our blindness to the fourth dimension may simply be the price of being alive.
