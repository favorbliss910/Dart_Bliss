## Part 4 — Music & Score for Narrated Shorts (601–800)

### The score's role under narration

601. **Narration is the melody** — Treat the voiceover as the lead line; the score accompanies and never carries a competing melody while a sentence is being spoken.
602. **Score the subtext** — Let music say what the narrator doesn't (the drongo's insincerity, the meerkats' naivety) so cues add a layer instead of repeating the words.
603. **Comedy needs a straight man** — When narration delivers a joke deadpan, keep the music sincere or neutral; winking music on top of a winking line kills the laugh.
604. **Phrases between sentences, not during** — Place melodic phrases, fills and motif statements in narration gaps of 0.4 s or longer; under words, hold rhythm or harmony only.
605. **Every cue has one job** — Before writing a cue, name its single function (establish place, reveal character, raise stakes, punctuate a joke) and cut any cue that has none.
606. **Silence is a cue** — Budget at least two deliberate music-free moments in the 3 minutes so the score's return feels like an event.
607. **Score the arc, not the shots** — Map cue intensity to the four story beats (setup, con, danger, twist) so energy forms one deliberate shape across the short.
608. **Hook in the first second** — Start music on frame one with a recognizable motif fragment or rhythmic figure; never fade in, because Shorts viewers decide to swipe within 2 seconds.
609. **Phone-first writing** — Compose so every idea survives a 1-inch speaker: character lives in melody and rhythm above 300 Hz, while sub-bass is decoration only.
610. **Declare the genre instantly** — Make the opening cue unmistakably comic (major, staccato, bouncy) so viewers read predators and theft as playful rather than grim.
611. **Don't say it twice** — If the narrator says "danger", shade rather than shout (lower register, thinner texture), and save full alarm music for threats the narration doesn't announce.
612. **The bed should go unnoticed** — If test listeners recall the underscore over the line it sat under, simplify the bed until they don't.

### Genre and instrument choices

613. **Pizzicato-and-marimba core** — Build the score on pizzicato strings, marimba, kalimba-like plucks and light hand percussion; together they read instantly as "mischievous nature documentary".
614. **Evoke the Kalahari lightly** — Suggest southern Africa with mbira/kalimba-style plucks, shakers and hand drums used as colour, never by imitating specific traditional songs.
615. **Nasal bass is funny bass** — Give the low comic line a reedy staccato bassoon or tuba-like timbre; a round sine bass reads serious, a buzzy one reads funny.
616. **Assign timbre to character** — Drongo gets a nasal clarinet or muted-trumpet lead, meerkats get marimba and woodblock, the hawk gets low brass pad, low strings and toms.
617. **Keep the band small** — Limit any moment to 4–5 simultaneous voices; a chamber-sized ensemble stays legible on phones and leaves space for narration.
618. **Low woodwind for tiptoeing** — Use staccato low clarinet or bassoon in chromatic steps for sneaking; it is the most universally understood sneak sound.
619. **Glockenspiel for treasure only** — Reserve glock or celesta for food reveals (a fat beetle, a stolen scorpion); used everywhere it turns the score childish.
620. **Percussion with personality** — Prefer woodblock, temple blocks, claves, shaker and slapstick over a drum kit; they are comic, articulate and sit above the voice's body.
621. **No four-on-the-floor** — Avoid a kick on every beat; it pushes the score toward advert/EDM territory and puts constant low thumps under every consonant.
622. **Walking bass for routine** — Use a staccato root–fifth walking bass for the meerkats' busy foraging; it signals bustling normality without needing a melody.
623. **Low brass only for real threat** — Hold low brass pads and timpani exclusively for the hawk so their first appearance instantly means danger.
624. **Whistle as the mimic's voice** — Use a pure whistle-like lead for the drongo's theme and fake alarms, echoing the real bird's whistled mimicry.
625. **One signature colour per section** — Don't stack ukulele, whistle and glock in one bed; give each section a single featured colour so the score has identity.
626. **Pads as glue, not wallpaper** — Use soft pads only in tension or emotional moments and at least 12 dB under the featured parts, never as a constant bed.
627. **Save legato strings for heart** — Introduce a sustained string texture only at the post-twist reflection so it lands as a tonal shift after minutes of plucking.
628. **Register equals body size** — Write meerkats around C5–C6, the drongo slightly above (D5–A6) with leaps, and the hawk below everything (C2–C3).
629. **Keep the palette for the series** — Reuse the same instrument set and character timbres in later episodes so the show is recognizable mid-scroll.
630. **Comedy colours are a limited budget** — Allow novelty colours (slide whistle, vibraslap, kazoo buzz) at most twice each per short so they stay funny.

### Key, mode and tempo

631. **Home key F or G major** — Set the main theme in F or G major so melodies sit roughly 350–800 Hz, clear of a male narrator's fundamental and below sibilance.
632. **Mixolydian for swagger** — Use Mixolydian's flat 7th (G Mixolydian with F natural) for the drongo's strut; it stays major but sounds roguish.
633. **Dorian for scheming** — Switch to D Dorian for planning and sneaking; the raised 6th keeps minor-mode sneakiness from sounding sad.
634. **Phrygian or diminished for danger** — Build the hawk cue on E Phrygian's flat 2nd or the half-whole diminished scale for menace that stops short of horror.
635. **Chromatic lower neighbours** — Approach melodic target notes from a semitone below; it is the cheapest, clearest "sneaky" signifier.
636. **Whole-tone for confusion** — Use a whole-tone run or wash when the meerkats are fooled and scatter; its missing tonal centre sounds like bewilderment.
637. **Base groove around 104 BPM** — Set the main comic groove between 100 and 112 BPM; jaunty enough for comedy, slow enough for gaps that fit ~150-wpm narration.
638. **Tiptoe tempo 84–92 BPM** — Drop to about 88 BPM with staccato eighths for sneaking scenes so steps feel careful but still rhythmic.
639. **Chase tempo 132–144 BPM** — Use about 138 BPM for scrambles and hawk dives, kept under 10 seconds because fast music crowds the narration.
640. **Related tempos, not random ones** — Relate section tempos by simple ratios (92 to 138 is 2:3) so transitions can pivot on a shared pulse.
641. **Swing for cheek** — Apply 55–62% swing to drongo cues and keep meerkat routine straight, so the characters contrast rhythmically.
642. **Step up to escalate** — Modulate up a whole step (F to G) as the con escalates, executing the change only inside a narration gap.
643. **Relative minor for consequences** — Move to the relative minor (F major to D minor) when the meerkats lose their food, keeping the palette so it feels like the same world.
644. **Lydian for the desert dawn** — Use the raised 4th (F Lydian's B natural) for the opening landscape; it reads as bright, open and wondrous.
645. **Choose keys around the narrator's pitch** — Measure the narrator's median pitch (often ~110 Hz male, ~200 Hz female) and avoid sustaining chord tones at that pitch or its octave under speech.
646. **Twist in a remote key** — Put the reveal in a key a major third away (F to A major) so the harmonic surprise matches the story surprise.

### Motifs and leitmotifs

647. **Three themes, three contours** — Give the drongo a zigzag, the meerkats bouncing repeated notes and the hawk a slow descent, so themes are distinguishable by shape alone.
648. **Motifs of 3–5 notes** — Keep each leitmotif to 3–5 notes within 1–2 bars so it fits in narration gaps and stays recognizable on a phone.
649. **Drongo theme: sly zigzag** — Write it as a rising fourth, a chromatic step down, a rest, then a grace-note flourish (e.g., C–F–E, rest, D♯–E).
650. **Meerkat theme: nervous repeats** — Build it from staccato sixteenths on one pitch ending in a quick upward step, like scurrying and popping upright.
651. **Hawk theme: slow falling interval** — Base it on a minor second or tritone falling over 2 bars in low register, followed by long silence.
652. **Sentinel sub-motif** — Give the meerkat on guard a perky rising-fourth "lookout" figure on marimba that returns every time a sentinel stands up.
653. **One alarm figure for real and fake** — Use the identical alarm figure (rapid repeated high minor third) for real and fake alarms so the audience is fooled along with the meerkats.
654. **Introduce themes nearly solo** — State each motif clearly, with minimal accompaniment, on the character's first appearance so the association is learned once.
655. **Then vary, never repeat verbatim** — After the introduction, change rhythm, register, instrument or harmony each time; repetition reads as a loop, variation reads as story.
656. **Motif as hidden commentary** — Quote the drongo motif quietly under meerkat scenes to hint he is watching before the narration reveals it.
657. **Shared interval foreshadows the twist** — Make the drongo theme and sentinel motif share the rising fourth so "guard is thief" is planted in the music.
658. **Invert for betrayal** — Flip the sentinel's rising fourth into a falling fourth when the drongo exploits the guard role.
659. **Augment for gravity, diminute for silliness** — Play a motif at half speed in low register to make it serious, at double speed an octave up to make it ridiculous.
660. **Impersonation by instrument swap** — When the drongo mimics a meerkat call, play the meerkat motif on the drongo's instrument.
661. **Title theme is the drongo theme** — Build the show's signature melody from the drongo motif, since he is the star and the subject of the twist.
662. **Withhold the last note for suspense** — Drop a motif's final note during tension; the ear's need for resolution keeps viewers watching.
663. **Pay off the withheld note** — Deliver that missing final note exactly on the payoff line for satisfying closure.
664. **Hide motifs in the bass** — State motifs in the bass under narration; the low register stays recognizable without fighting the voice's midrange.
665. **One motif at a time** — Never stack two leitmotifs at equal level except in one deliberate climactic counterpoint; on phones it becomes mush.
666. **Counterpoint the con once** — At the heist peak, combine meerkat theme (high) and drongo theme (low) a single time so both sides are heard in one moment.
667. **Fit motifs to measured gaps** — Measure narration gaps first, then trim statements to fit (a 0.9 s gap holds three eighth notes at 104 BPM, with release).
668. **Teach the drongo theme early** — Sound the drongo motif at least twice in the first 15 seconds so it is learned before the plot depends on it.

### Arrangement density and room for the voice

669. **Sparse under speech, bloom in gaps** — Drop to 1–2 active parts under narration and expand to 4–5 in pauses longer than about 1 second.
670. **Rhythm under, melody around** — Under lines, use staccato chords and bass; give sustained lead melody only the spaces between sentences.
671. **Short notes beat long notes** — Prefer notes decaying under 200 ms in the voice's register; sustained mid notes mask vowels continuously.
672. **Write rests on purpose** — Leave at least one beat of rest per bar in any lead part during narrated passages.
673. **No wall-to-wall sixteenths** — Avoid constant sixteenth-note shakers or hats under speech; use eighths, or keep sixteenths very quiet and dark.
674. **Thin the chords** — Voice harmony as 2–3 notes (root, 3rd, 7th shells) under speech instead of full triads with doublings.
675. **Open low, close high** — Keep chord tones below 250 Hz at least a fifth apart to avoid mud, clustering tighter only above 1 kHz.
676. **Short fragments, long rests** — Under narration, limit any melodic fragment to 2 beats followed by at least 2 beats of rest.
677. **Density ladder** — Define levels 0–4 (silence, pulse, +bass, +harmony, +melody) and step through them instead of flipping between all-on and all-off.
678. **Energy through rhythm, not volume** — Raise excitement by adding subdivision and register, not level, so loudness stays predictable under the voice.
679. **Thin out before punchlines** — Strip to near-silence one bar before a punchline so the line lands in space.
680. **Change one layer per section** — Swap or add at least one layer at each story section so progression is audible even at low volume.
681. **Short tails under speech** — Keep reverb decay on music under 1.2 s during narration; long tails fill the gaps the voice needs.
682. **Align with or clearly avoid stressed syllables** — Match groove accents to the narrator's stressed syllables on key lines or offset them by a clear half beat, never a near-miss.
683. **Enter after the sentence** — Hold new instruments until the narrator finishes a sentence and bring them in on the next downbeat.
684. **Subtract to fit** — Write the cue as if there were no narration, then delete parts until the voice fits; deletion yields cleaner beds than layering up.

### Frequency slots the music must avoid

685. **Leave 1–4 kHz to consonants** — Under narration, pick or filter music timbres to carry little energy in 1–4 kHz (e.g., lowpass plucks near 2 kHz).
686. **Avoid the voice's body band** — Don't sustain notes in 90–250 Hz under a male narrator, or 165–300 Hz under a female one; pads there swallow the voice's warmth.
687. **Keep off the sibilance band** — Keep shakers and cymbals out of 5–8 kHz under "s"-heavy lines, either removed or highpassed above 9 kHz.
688. **Hooks below 300 Hz under speech** — Under lines put melodic hooks in the bass; let them climb into 500 Hz–3 kHz only inside gaps.
689. **Give bass audible harmonics** — Ensure bass lines have energy at 200–600 Hz (saw content or saturation), since phone speakers roll off below about 200 Hz.
690. **Nothing useful below 40 Hz** — Highpass music at 35–40 Hz; sub-sonic content wastes headroom and phones cannot reproduce it.
691. **Kick knock, not click** — Shape kicks with body at 60–80 Hz and a 700 Hz–1 kHz knock instead of a 2–4 kHz beater click that fights consonants.
692. **Marimba overlaps the voice** — Under speech keep marimba soft and short (decay under 300 ms), saving low-mid rolls and sustained tremolo for gaps.
693. **Pads at the edges** — Keep pads either dark (lowpassed near 800 Hz, 12 dB under the voice) or airy (highpassed above 6 kHz), never centred on speech formants.
694. **One instrument per band** — Give each instrument a home band (bass 60–250 Hz, marimba 500 Hz–2 kHz, whistle 1.5–3 kHz, shaker 8–12 kHz) and avoid sharing bands simultaneously.
695. **Speech-safe bar variants** — Write alternate versions of busy bars where midrange parts drop out or jump an octave, and use them wherever a line overlaps.
696. **Watch 300–500 Hz mud** — Don't stack low pizzicato, low marimba and bass harmonics in 300–500 Hz; it makes the narrator sound boxy on phones.

### Stingers, hits and mickey-mousing

697. **Mickey-mouse sparingly** — Sync music to action for only 3–6 key gags per short; constant mickey-mousing exhausts viewers and feels like parody.
698. **Land hits within a frame** — Place each stinger's transient within ±1 frame (±42 ms at 24 fps) of the visual impact.
699. **Anticipate the hit** — Precede big hits with a 200–500 ms pickup or upward rip so the impact feels earned.
700. **Stingers under 1.5 seconds** — Keep comic stingers 0.3–1.5 s; longer ones become cues and collide with the next line.
701. **Stingers in the cue's key** — Write stingers diatonic to the running cue, or deliberately sour, so they stitch into the score instead of sounding pasted on.
702. **Sour cluster for failure** — Use a minor-second clash (C with D♭) for meerkat mishaps; the wrong harmony is the joke.
703. **Skip the sad trombone** — Avoid the stock descending "wah-wah" trombone; write a bespoke falling figure on the relevant character's instrument.
704. **Pizzicato footsteps** — Mickey the drongo's sneaking steps with one pizz note per step, rising chromatically, stopping exactly when he freezes.
705. **Hit, then silence** — After a big hit, cut music for 300–800 ms so both the hit and the reaction read.
706. **Music or SFX leads, not both** — On each impact, decide whether the stinger or the sound effect carries it, and keep the other clearly secondary.
707. **Glissandi for vertical motion** — Use a fast 8-note upward marimba or harp gliss (~250 ms) for a meerkat popping upright, downward for diving into a burrow.
708. **Pick tempo from the action grid** — Choose BPM so key actions land on beats: BPM = 60 × fps ÷ frames-per-beat (24 fps, 14 frames ≈ 103 BPM).
709. **Never hit a stressed word** — Move a stinger off a stressed narration syllable by up to a beat into the gap, or cut it.
710. **Stingers derived from motifs** — Build each character's stinger from their motif's last two notes so every hit also reinforces identity.
711. **Three tiers of hit** — Use a tick (single note), hit (chord plus percussion) or slam (full band plus cymbal), matching size to gag; most gags deserve ticks.
712. **Fermata on freeze-frames** — For freeze-frames and reaction takes, hold one sustained note or chord until the image moves again.

### Stops, dropouts and restarts

713. **Stop on a beat with a button** — End music with a staccato tutti or choked cymbal on a beat rather than a fade, so the stop reads as intentional.
714. **Drop out before key lines** — Cut music 0.5–1 bar before an important line so it plays in silence.
715. **Restart on the next downbeat** — Re-enter on the first downbeat at least 200 ms after the narrator's last syllable.
716. **Restart with a pickup** — Return with a one-beat pickup figure so the re-entry feels like continuation, not a jump.
717. **Silence the punchline, restart on the reaction** — Drop the score for the punchline and bring it back on the character's reaction shot.
718. **Near-silence when silence feels broken** — Where total silence would read as a glitch, leave one held tone or a soft ticking woodblock instead.
719. **No off-beat mid-phrase cuts** — Never stop mid-phrase off the beat unless a gag motivates it; on phones it reads as buffering.
720. **Return changed** — When music comes back after a dropout, change key, instrument or tempo so the dropout marks a story turn.
721. **Let stops ring** — Allow a stop's final hit 0.8–1.5 s of natural decay or reverb rather than a hard digital cutoff.
722. **Ration dropouts** — Limit deliberate dropouts to 3–5 per short so the score feels shaped rather than broken.

### Transitions between story sections

723. **Change cues on bar lines** — Align cue changes to bar boundaries at scene cuts, nudging the cut or the cue by up to one beat so they meet.
724. **Pivot on a common tone** — Bridge two cues with a held note belonging to both keys (A links F major and D minor).
725. **Fill bridges tempo changes** — Use a one-bar percussion fill whose final beat already plays at the new tempo.
726. **Riser into a section** — Lead into a new section with a 1–2 bar tremolo or filtered-noise riser that ends exactly on its downbeat.
727. **Reverse swell over a cut** — Use a 0.5–1 s reversed cymbal or pad swell into a new cue to smooth hard visual cuts.
728. **Metric modulation** — Move from 92 to 138 BPM by making old triplet eighths the new straight eighths, so the shift feels natural.
729. **Transitions live in gaps** — Time section changes to land in narration pauses, never mid-sentence.
730. **Stinger doubling as transition** — Close a section with a character stinger that also announces the next scene.
731. **Crossfade only when compatible** — Crossfade cues only if they share key and tempo; otherwise cut on a hit or through silence.
732. **Open with identity** — Start each new section with its defining instrument on beat one so the change registers immediately.

### Building tension

733. **Ostinato breeds unease** — Run a one-bar staccato ostinato unchanged while other layers enter; persistence itself builds tension.
734. **Climb by semitones** — Raise the ostinato a semitone every 2 bars as danger nears.
735. **Pedal point** — Hold a low tonic pedal while harmonies above shift into dissonance, grounding the suspense.
736. **Subtle accelerando** — Speed up 5–10 BPM over 8 bars into a climax; it is felt more than consciously heard.
737. **Tremolo for the approach** — Use 32nd-note tremolo in high strings or pads as the hawk's shadow approaches.
738. **Shrink the intervals** — Narrow melodic steps to semitones as danger grows, and widen them again once safe.
739. **Remove the floor** — Pull the bass out before danger strikes to create a floating, unstable feeling.
740. **Half-time heartbeat** — Add a soft low double-pulse at half the current tempo under suspense scenes.
741. **Silence before the strike** — Cut all music one beat before the hawk dives so the attack erupts out of silence.
742. **Release fully** — After danger passes, resolve to the tonic with a lighter texture so viewers exhale; unreleased tension tires them.

### The twist and reveal cue

743. **Plant the twist early** — Sound the shared drongo–sentinel rising fourth in the first 30 seconds so the twist feels inevitable in hindsight.
744. **Clear the air before the line** — Drop all music one bar before "The best guard in the desert is also its best thief."
745. **One chord on "thief"** — Land a single bright chord a major third from home (F to A major) on the word "thief".
746. **Deceptive cadence for misdirection** — Set up the reveal with V and resolve to ♭VI instead of I, mirroring the story's sleight of hand.
747. **Sentinel motif on drongo timbre** — Play the guard's lookout figure on the drongo's instrument during the reveal to show they are one.
748. **Drongo theme as victory lap** — After the twist line, give the drongo theme its fullest arrangement exactly once.
749. **Half-time reveal** — Halve the tempo for the reveal so it breathes, then snap back to base tempo for the button.
750. **Wink, don't moralize** — Keep the reveal cue playful (major, staccato, a sly grace note) rather than ominous; it is a comedy twist.
751. **Frame-accurate reveal** — Place the reveal chord's attack on the stressed syllable of "thief" within ±1 frame.
752. **Widest texture after the quietest moment** — Contrast the near-silence before the reveal with the score's fullest arrangement after it.

### The ending button

753. **Button, never fade** — End on a short, decisive staccato tonic chord (V–I or ♭VII–I) rather than a fade-out.
754. **Button from the drongo motif** — Build the final button from the drongo motif's last two notes plus a tonic hit.
755. **One beat of air** — Leave a one-beat rest before the final hit; the space makes it punchier.
756. **Cheeky cadence over cliché** — Use a flat-7 to tonic or a grace-note flourish instead of the overused "shave and a haircut" ending.
757. **Sync with the last image** — Land the button on the final cut, wink or freeze of the drongo.
758. **Audible on phones** — Keep the button's top note between 500 Hz and 2 kHz so it reads clearly on small speakers.
759. **After the final word** — Place the button at least 300 ms after the last spoken syllable so it never clips the line.
760. **Short ring-out** — Let the button ring 0.5–1.0 s and end cleanly so the loop restart isn't delayed.

### Loopability

761. **Design the loop seam** — Make the ending lead naturally back into the opening, since Shorts and TikTok autoplay and replays count.
762. **Same key and tempo at both ends** — End in the opening's key and tempo so the loop never jars.
763. **Opening answers the button** — Write the first bar so it sounds like a natural reply to the final button.
764. **No tails across the seam** — Resolve or truncate reverb tails within the last 200 ms so they don't clash with the restart.
765. **Whole bars only** — Make total music length an exact number of bars at the opening tempo so a loop lands on a downbeat.
766. **Audition the seam ten times** — Loop the last 5 s into the first 5 s ten times; fix anything that still sounds like an edit.

### Ducking from the music's point of view

767. **Write to survive ducking** — Compose beds that still sound complete at −10 dB; parts that only work loud vanish under the voice.
768. **Expect 8–12 dB of duck** — Arrange so key rhythmic elements remain audible when the music sits 8–12 dB lower under narration.
769. **Respect duck timing** — Assume roughly 50–150 ms attack and 300–600 ms release, and don't start motifs the instant a line ends while the music is still recovering.
770. **Decaying notes don't pump** — Prefer decaying notes over sustained pads under frequent duck/release cycles, which make pads audibly pump.
771. **Pre-duck before the first word** — Begin ducking 100–200 ms before a line starts so the narrator's first consonant is clear.
772. **Band-limited duck for busy cues** — For dense cues, duck only the 1–4 kHz band by about 6 dB, keeping bass and air intact.
773. **Exempt gap stingers** — Keep stingers and the final button, which live in gaps, out of the ducking path so they hit at full size.
774. **Hold through short gaps** — If a narration gap is under 400 ms, keep the music ducked rather than letting it swell and drop again.

### Humanization

775. **Onset jitter** — Randomize note onsets with Gaussian jitter (σ ≈ 5–10 ms), looser for plucks and tighter for percussion.
776. **Metric velocity accents** — Shape velocity by beat position (downbeats +15%, offbeats −10%) and add ±10% random variation on top.
777. **Phrase dynamics** — Crescendo into phrase peaks and taper the last notes of phrases by 3–6 dB.
778. **Round-robin variation** — Vary repeated notes by ±5 cents pitch, ±10% filter cutoff and ±15% decay to avoid machine-gun repeats.
779. **Slow tuning drift** — Detune sustained voices ±3–8 cents with slow LFOs at 0.1–0.5 Hz.
780. **Breathing swing** — Vary swing amount by ±3% from bar to bar rather than holding it fixed.
781. **Phrase-end ritardando** — Slow by 3–8% over the final beat of key phrases and before the button.
782. **Ghost notes** — Add percussion ghost notes at 20–35% velocity between main hits for a played feel.
783. **Varied note lengths** — Randomize pizzicato and marimba note durations by ±20% so articulation feels hand-played.
784. **Character feel through placement** — Place drongo lead notes 10–20 ms late for laid-back swagger and meerkat notes 5–10 ms early for nervous energy.
785. **Seeded randomness** — Use a fixed random seed per cue so humanized renders are reproducible across revisions.
786. **Imperfect unisons** — When doubling a line, offset the double by 8–20 ms and 5–10 cents so it sounds like two players, not one copy.

### Synthesizing convincing instruments in code

787. **Karplus–Strong for plucks** — Make pizzicato and kalimba plucks with a noise burst into a delay of sampleRate/frequency samples, lowpass-averaged with 0.990–0.998 feedback.
788. **Modal marimba** — Sum sine partials at 1 : 4 : 10 frequency ratios with decays near 0.6 s, 0.15 s and 0.05 s, plus a 1–2 ms mallet click.
789. **Kalimba tines** — Combine a sine fundamental with a weak inharmonic partial around 5–6× that decays within 50 ms, plus a faint buzz for tine character.
790. **Pizzicato envelope** — Use 2–5 ms attack, 150–400 ms exponential decay, and a lowpass falling from about 4 kHz to 800 Hz during the decay.
791. **Comic bass** — Run a saw or square through a resonant 24 dB/oct lowpass at 400–800 Hz with a short filter envelope for a bassoon/tuba-like bark.
792. **Noise-born percussion** — Make shakers from 30–60 ms highpassed noise bursts (6–12 kHz) and woodblocks from 800–1200 Hz damped sines decaying in 20–40 ms.
793. **Whistle lead** — Use a sine with 5–6 Hz vibrato of ±15–25 cents that fades in after 150 ms, plus a touch of bandpassed breath noise.
794. **Clarinet-like reed** — Use a square or pulse wave (odd harmonics) lowpassed near 1.5–2 kHz with gentle delayed vibrato.
795. **Brass-like swell** — Use a saw whose lowpass opens over 50–80 ms after onset, since brass brightens as the note speaks.
796. **Detuned-saw pads** — Stack 3–7 saws detuned ±7–15 cents, lowpass at 1.5–2.5 kHz, with 300–800 ms attack.
797. **Toms and timpani** — Synthesize with a sine sweeping from 1.5× to 1× its pitch over 50–100 ms, mixed with a short noise transient.
798. **Band-limited oscillators** — Use PolyBLEP or 2–4× oversampling for saws and squares so high notes don't alias.
799. **Click-free envelopes** — Give every note at least 1–2 ms attack and release ramps to prevent clicks at note boundaries.
800. **Velocity controls brightness** — Map velocity to filter cutoff (+30–50% at maximum) as well as amplitude, because real instruments brighten when played harder.
