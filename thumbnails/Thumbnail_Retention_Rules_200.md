# 200 Rules of Retention Thumbnail Design

## One Subject and Instant Read

1. **One hero only** — Build every thumbnail around a single dominant subject covering at least 35% of the frame; every other element exists only to support it.
2. **100 ms flash test** — Flash the image for 100 ms to someone who has not seen the episode; if they cannot name the subject and the emotion, redesign.
3. **Judge at feed size** — Evaluate the design at the size it appears in a phone feed, about 180×320 px for 9:16 and 360×200 px for 16:9, never at full resolution.
4. **Three-element cap** — Limit the image to three readable elements at most: the shocked face, the threat and the curiosity object.
5. **Blur test** — Apply a 10 px blur at 1080 px width; the hero's silhouette and the brightest area must still read as one clear shape.
6. **Grayscale test** — Desaturate the image; if the subject does not separate from the background by value alone, fix values before touching colour.
7. **Single first fixation** — Make the hero's eyes the first place the eye lands, followed by exactly one secondary point, never two competing first reads.
8. **Delete small clutter** — Remove any background prop smaller than 3% of the frame that does not advance the story, because it becomes noise at feed size.
9. **Clean subject boundary** — Keep at least 8 px (at 1080 px width) of plain contrasting background between the hero's outline and any busy shape.
10. **Five-word description** — Be able to describe the thumbnail in five words or fewer, such as "terrified girl, whispering librarian behind".
11. **Shorthand setting** — Make the location identifiable from one or two signature shapes, such as towering shelves, instead of detailed environment painting.
12. **Minimum head size** — Never let the hero's head fall below 20% of frame height on 9:16 or 30% of frame height on 16:9.
13. **Scroll-speed assumption** — Design for viewers flicking past 2 to 3 cards per second, so the image must be understood in one glance without reading.
14. **Clear what, mysterious why** — Keep what is happening visually unambiguous and reserve all mystery for why it is happening.
15. **Real-phone check** — Before publishing, view the image on a real phone at 50% screen brightness inside a feed mockup and a search-results mockup.

## Faces and Expression

16. **Face is the hook** — Make the shocked face the largest element, filling 25 to 40% of the 9:16 canvas height.
17. **Full-intensity shock** — Push expressions to maximum: raised brows, enlarged eyes and open mouth together, never a mild or ambiguous reaction.
18. **Enlarge eye discs** — Scale the white eye discs to 130 to 150% of their normal episode size so they read at feed size.
19. **Pupil size by emotion** — Shrink dot pupils to about 15% of the eye-disc diameter for terror and keep them near 30% for wonder or curiosity.
20. **Pupils point the way** — Offset both pupils toward the threat or the book so the viewer's eye follows the character's gaze.
21. **Direct eye contact** — For being-watched stories, aim both pupils dead centre at the lens to create eye contact that stops the scroll.
22. **One gaze mode per image** — Use either eye contact or gaze toward the threat, never a mix, unless a deliberately split, panicked look is the point.
23. **Gasp mouth** — Draw the shock mouth as a solid dark vertical oval at least 1.5 times taller than wide for maximum contrast on the white head.
24. **Ration the scream** — Reserve the wide jagged scream mouth for peak-terror episodes, using it in no more than one of every four thumbnails.
25. **Secret mouth** — For secrecy stories, use a small round "o" mouth or a finger-to-lips gesture to signal a secret rather than a jump scare.
26. **Hands on face** — Press both hands to the cheeks or over the mouth to amplify shock, with hands touching the head outline, not floating near it.
27. **Fear brows** — Add two thick brow strokes above the eye discs, inner ends tilted up 20 to 30 degrees, to lock in fear.
28. **Fear accents** — Add one to three sweat drops or short tremble lines beside the head when the face alone reads as borderline.
29. **Recoil tilt** — Tilt the head 10 to 15 degrees away from the threat to suggest recoil and add diagonal energy.
30. **Brightest face** — Keep the face the highest-value area in the image, because a white head is the strongest natural focal point.
31. **Unobstructed features** — Never let hair, hands, props or overlays cover more than 20% of the eyes and mouth combined.
32. **Mismatched second face** — If a second face appears, give it a contrasting emotion, such as a calm smile, so the mismatch creates tension.
33. **Emotion fits genre** — Use fear for mystery-threat episodes and awe with sparkle highlights for nature-wonder twists so the expectation matches the payoff.

## Character Design for Thumbnails

34. **Silhouette first** — Pose characters so the pose, filled solid black, still communicates the action and emotion.
35. **Separate the limbs** — Keep stick arms and legs clear of the torso with visible negative space so the pose reads at 180 px wide.
36. **Thicker outlines** — Raise outline weight to about 1.5 times the episode line, roughly 12 to 16 px at 1080 px width.
37. **Head-heavy proportions** — Enlarge the head to up to 50% of total body height in close-ups so the face dominates.
38. **Bust crop by default** — Frame head and shoulders unless the full-body pose itself carries the story.
39. **Line of action** — Use a leaning, recoiling or frozen mid-step pose with one clear curve of action, never a neutral standing stance.
40. **Stay on model** — Match head shape, eye style, outfit colours and accessories to the episode so viewers instantly recognise the character in the video.
41. **Signature accessory** — Give each recurring character one bold identifier, such as a red scarf, large enough to read at feed size.
42. **Location costume cue** — Add one costume cue from the episode, such as a patterned wool sweater for Norway, to signal place without text.
43. **Distinct antagonist shape** — Give threatening characters an outline that contrasts sharply with the round hero, such as an elongated body or hunched shoulders.
44. **Restrained painterly shading** — Limit brush texture on characters to two or three value steps so shading never muddies the silhouette.
45. **Repaint, don't grab** — Repaint or retouch the key frame rather than exporting a raw frame, then place that polished art as frame 1 of the video.
46. **No off-model hype** — Never make characters scarier, older or visually different from how they appear in the episode.

## Props and the Object of Curiosity

47. **One hero object** — Feature exactly one curiosity object, here the blank book, as the second-largest element after the face.
48. **Object scale** — Size the book to at least 15% of the frame so its blank pages are readable at feed size.
49. **Show the impossibility** — Make the strange detail visible, such as pure white empty pages, because a normal-looking book raises no question.
50. **Pages to camera** — Angle the open book 20 to 40 degrees toward the lens so the empty pages face the viewer.
51. **Object as light source** — Make the pages emit a soft glow so the object becomes both a light source and the second focal point.
52. **Physical link** — Show the hero's hand holding or touching the book to connect the person and the mystery in one action.
53. **Bold prop shapes** — Draw props with simple bold shapes and no fine lettering or ornament that disappears below 200 px.
54. **Unexpected state** — Show the object doing something wrong, such as pages lifting or one dark word appearing, to hint at the twist.
55. **Repetition as rhythm** — Render repeated objects, such as shelves of blank books, as a simple background pattern rather than individual details.
56. **Converging lines** — Use gaze, arms, shelf edges or light rays as leading lines that converge on the book.
57. **Object colour contrast** — Colour the object in a hue that contrasts with both the background and the hero's outfit.
58. **Object on screen early** — Ensure the featured object is in the first frame and stays visible through at least the first 3 seconds.
59. **Nature-story swap** — In nature episodes, replace the book with the single strangest natural element, such as a glowing mushroom, applying the same scale and glow rules.

## Threat, Stakes and the Curiosity Gap

60. **Threat right behind** — Place the threat directly behind the hero, overlapping or within one head-width of their shoulder, so danger feels immediate.
61. **Decide who knows** — Choose deliberately between a hero who has not seen the threat (dramatic irony) and one who just has (shock), never both.
62. **Partial reveal** — Show only 30 to 60% of the threat, such as a silhouette, half a face or eyes in darkness, so the rest requires watching.
63. **Shadow stand-in** — Cast the threat's shadow over the hero or wall when showing the threat itself would spoil the twist.
64. **Eyes in the dark** — Use two small bright eye shapes in a dark area as a cheap threat signal that reads at any size.
65. **The whisper moment** — For the library story, freeze the librarian leaning toward the hero's ear a split second before the secret is spoken.
66. **Imply, never spoil** — Show the setup that makes the twist inevitable, never the twist itself.
67. **Exactly one question** — Design the image to raise one specific question, such as "What did she whisper?", that the video answers.
68. **Visible stakes** — Show what the hero could lose, such as an exit closing behind them, through pose and environment rather than words.
69. **Looming scale** — Draw the threat 1.5 to 3 times the hero's size, or towering over them, to convey a power imbalance.
70. **Wrongness detail** — Add one small "something is off" detail, such as a clock with no hands, that rewards a second look.
71. **Freeze before impact** — Depict the instant before the event, not during or after, because anticipation outperforms resolution.
72. **Calm threat, panicked hero** — Pair a calm or smiling threat with a terrified hero, as the contrast unsettles more than two scared faces.
73. **Genuine threat** — Use only a threat that is a real story element, never a decoy creature or scene invented for the thumbnail.
74. **Danger without gore** — Signal danger through posture, shadow and light, never blood or injury, to stay all-ages and advertiser-friendly.
75. **Narration extends the gap** — Make the first narrated line deepen the image's question rather than answer it.

## Composition

76. **Shorts canvas** — Build 9:16 thumbnails and first frames at 1080×1920 px.
77. **Long-form canvas** — Build the 16:9 thumbnail at 1920×1080 px and export at no less than 1280×720 px.
78. **Right-side Shorts buttons** — Keep essential elements out of the right 140 px of a 1080×1920 frame, where like, comment and share buttons sit.
79. **Bottom caption overlay** — Keep face and object above the bottom 420 px of a 1080×1920 frame, where title, channel name and sound info overlay.
80. **Top overlay band** — Leave the top 200 px free of essential detail to clear the status bar, search icon and TikTok's Following/For You tabs.
81. **Central safe box** — Place all essential elements within roughly x 60 to 940 and y 200 to 1500 on a 1080×1920 frame.
82. **Grid crop survival** — Keep face and object inside the central 1080×1440 px (3:4) area so they survive profile-grid crops on TikTok and channel pages.
83. **Timestamp corner** — Keep the bottom-right 18% of width and 15% of height clear on 16:9, where the duration badge covers the image.
84. **Progress-bar strip** — Keep the bottom 5% of the 16:9 frame free of key details, where the red watched-progress bar appears.
85. **Rule of thirds** — Put the hero's eyes on an upper third intersection and the threat on the opposite third line.
86. **Scale contrast** — Make the nearest element at least 3 times larger than the farthest so depth reads instantly.
87. **Three depth layers** — Stack the face in the foreground, the threat in the midground and the setting in the background.
88. **Close to the lens** — Frame the hero's face as if within arm's reach of the camera, accepting a crop of the head top.
89. **Crop with intent** — Crop limbs between joints, and let the threat run off the frame edge to suggest it is bigger than shown.
90. **Eyes stay in frame** — Crop the top of the head freely, but keep both eye discs and the mouth fully inside the frame.
91. **Diagonal arrangement** — Arrange face, object and threat along a diagonal rather than a flat horizontal row.
92. **Quiet space** — Leave at least 15% of the frame as low-detail space so the subject pops.
93. **Recompose for 16:9** — Rebuild the 16:9 layout instead of cropping the 9:16, spreading the face left and the threat right across the wider frame.
94. **Camera angle** — Shoot the threat from slightly below so it looms and the hero at eye level to keep empathy.
95. **Frame within a frame** — Use shelves, doorways or arches to box the hero, adding depth without adding clutter.

## Colour, Value and Contrast

96. **Value separation** — Keep at least a 60% luminance difference between the hero's head and the area directly behind it.
97. **Dark ground, white heads** — Default to mid-to-dark backgrounds so the big white heads are the brightest shapes in the frame.
98. **Complementary pair** — Build each palette on one complementary pair, such as teal with orange or violet with yellow.
99. **Warm safety, cool danger** — Light the hero and the book warm and keep the threat in cool blues or greens.
100. **Three-hue limit** — Restrict the palette to three dominant hues plus the black outlines and white heads.
101. **Feed-level saturation** — Raise saturation 10 to 20% above the episode grade so the image competes with live-action content.
102. **Rim light** — Add a 4 to 8 px bright rim on the hero's edge facing the light to separate them from dark backgrounds.
103. **Unique threat accent** — Give the threat one saturated accent, such as red eyes, that appears nowhere else in the image.
104. **Out-colour the feed** — Check the top 20 results for the episode's keyword and choose a dominant colour none of them use.
105. **Dark-mode test** — Preview on a #0F0F0F background and keep frame edges above about 10% brightness so the thumbnail does not dissolve into dark mode.
106. **Light-mode test** — Preview on a #FFFFFF background and avoid near-white edges that merge into the page.
107. **Colour-blind check** — Run a deuteranopia simulation and ensure hero and threat differ in value, not just in red versus green.
108. **Grade continuity** — Match the thumbnail palette to the colour grade of the video's first 3 seconds so the opening feels seamless.
109. **Outlines guarantee separation** — Keep the thick black outline unbroken on every colour boundary so shapes separate even when hues are close.
110. **No muddy midtones** — Push shadows darker and highlights lighter until no large area sits in the 40 to 60% gray band.
111. **Location colour code** — Use a palette that evokes the setting, such as icy blue-white with amber lamplight for a Norwegian library.

## Lighting and Focus

112. **One motivated light** — Use a single clear key light with a story source, such as the glowing book or a lamp.
113. **Underlight for dread** — Light the threat from below so its face catches unnatural upward shadows.
114. **Contained bloom** — Keep the object's glow within 1.5 times its own size so it never washes out the face.
115. **Background depth of field** — Blur the background by 4 to 8 px at 1080 px width while keeping the hero razor sharp.
116. **Soft-focus threat** — Apply a light 2 to 3 px blur to a midground threat for mystery, while keeping its silhouette readable.
117. **Vignette** — Darken the outer edges by 20 to 35% to pull the eye toward the centre.
118. **Pool of light** — Place the hero in a pool of light surrounded by darkness to isolate them instantly.
119. **Pure white eyes** — Keep the eye discs at #FFFFFF, never shaded below 90% brightness, so they remain the sharpest highlight.
120. **Backlit doorway** — Backlight a threat in a doorway or window to produce a crisp silhouette with no need for detail.
121. **Consistent shadows** — Make every cast shadow agree with the key light direction so the image feels intentional, not pasted.
122. **Avoid faint gradients** — Avoid gradients with under 10% value change across large areas, as they vanish or band after compression.
123. **Anti-banding grain** — Add 1 to 2% noise to dark gradients before export to prevent compression banding.
124. **Sharpen after downscale** — Apply light sharpening only after resizing to the final export size.

## Text

125. **No text by default** — Let the image work alone and add text only when it supplies a fact the picture cannot show.
126. **Text that earns its place** — Use text only for a location, number or impossibility, such as "0 WORDS", that the art cannot convey.
127. **Three-word maximum** — Never exceed three words, and prefer one or two.
128. **Minimum text height** — Make capital letters at least 8% of frame height on 16:9 (about 58 px at 720 px) and 5% on 9:16 (about 96 px).
129. **Heavy sans-serif** — Use a bold or black-weight sans-serif at 800 weight or heavier, never script or thin fonts.
130. **Text contrast** — Give text a 6 to 10 px black stroke or hard shadow and a contrast ratio of at least 7:1 against its background.
131. **Keep text off faces** — Never place text over the eyes, mouth or the curiosity object.
132. **Text placement** — Place text in the upper safe area on 9:16 and the top-left on 16:9, well clear of the timestamp.
133. **Single text colour** — Use one text colour, typically white or yellow, with an optional single highlighted word.
134. **Add, don't echo** — Ensure any text adds information absent from both the image and the title.
135. **Separate text layer** — Keep text on its own layer so a clean, text-free version exists for localised or international uploads.
136. **Delay burned-in captions** — Start on-screen subtitles after the first 0.5 seconds so the opening frame stays identical to the thumbnail.

## Matching the First Frame and the Title

137. **Pixel-identical frame 1** — Use the exact thumbnail art as frame 1 of the video, with no extra overlays.
138. **Brief hold** — Hold the opening frame no longer than 0.5 seconds (12 frames at 24 fps) before motion begins.
139. **Motion continues the image** — Start the first movement from the thumbnail pose, such as the librarian leaning closer, within the first second.
140. **Audio on frame 1** — Begin narration or a sharp sound cue on the very first frame so silence does not invite a swipe.
141. **Set the cover manually** — Select the thumbnail frame as the Shorts thumbnail in the YouTube app and as the cover on TikTok rather than accepting the auto-pick.
142. **Title adds the missing half** — Let the image show who and what while the title supplies where or why.
143. **Never describe the image** — Do not title a frame of a scared girl and whispering librarian "Scared of the Librarian".
144. **Title sharpens the question** — Pair the whisper image with a title like "Every Book Here Is Blank" so image and title form one mystery.
145. **Shorts title length** — Keep Shorts titles under 40 characters so the overlay does not truncate them.
146. **Long-form title length** — Keep 16:9 titles under 60 characters with the key hook in the first 35.
147. **Hook line matches frame** — Make the first narrated sentence, spoken within 1 second, refer to something visible in the frame.
148. **Long-form promise timing** — Show the thumbnail moment in the long-form video within the first 60 seconds.
149. **Same moment across formats** — Build the 16:9 thumbnail from the same story moment as the Short so returning viewers recognise it.
150. **Seamless loop** — End Shorts on a frame that flows back into frame 1 so replays feel continuous and push average view percentage above 100%.

## Series Branding

151. **Style is the brand** — Never mix in photos, 3D renders or other art styles; the stick-figure look itself must identify the channel.
152. **Signature colour** — Include one recurring brand colour in every thumbnail, such as the rim light or glow hue.
153. **16:9 corner mark** — Place a small series icon, under 5% of the area, in the same top-left spot on long-form thumbnails only.
154. **No marks on Shorts** — Leave logos off 9:16 frames, where overlays and the scrolling context make them clutter.
155. **Layout templates** — Maintain two or three fixed layout templates and rotate them so the channel is recognisable but not repetitive.
156. **Avoid episode numbers** — Keep episode numbers out of the image, as they signal required prior viewing and suppress new viewers.
157. **Profile-grid harmony** — Review the latest nine thumbnails as a grid and vary the dominant colour so adjacent tiles differ.
158. **Location cue system** — Signal each country the same way every time, such as a landmark silhouette in the background or a flag-colour accent.
159. **Font lock** — Use one typeface for all thumbnail text across the series.
160. **Recurring motif** — Repeat one compositional signature, such as the hero's face always in the left third, across the series.

## A/B Testing and Metrics

161. **Use Test & Compare** — Upload up to three 16:9 variants with YouTube Studio's Test & Compare, which picks the winner by watch-time share.
162. **One variable per variant** — Change only one element per variant, such as gaze direction or background colour, so the result is attributable.
163. **Let tests finish** — Run each test until YouTube declares a result or for at least 14 days.
164. **Episode-level Shorts tests** — Since Shorts covers cannot be split-tested, alternate two design approaches across at least 10 episodes each and compare averages.
165. **Viewed vs swiped away** — Track YouTube Studio's "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts, aiming for 70% viewed and revising the first frame below 60%.
166. **Three-second retention** — Revise the opening frame and hook if more than 35% of viewers leave within the first 3 seconds.
167. **Relative CTR** — Judge long-form impressions CTR against the channel's own median rather than generic 4 to 10% benchmarks.
168. **CTR times retention** — Score thumbnails by CTR multiplied by average view duration, since high CTR with an early drop signals a broken promise.
169. **Thirty-second check** — Treat long-form 30-second retention below 70% as a sign the thumbnail and opening are misaligned.
170. **Loop signal** — Treat Shorts average percentage viewed above 100% as proof the first frame and loop are working.
171. **TikTok metrics** — Check TikTok's average watch time, full-watch percentage and For You traffic share for every post.
172. **Split by traffic source** — Compare CTR separately for browse, search and suggested, as each rewards different designs.
173. **Minimum sample** — Do not judge a thumbnail before 1,000 impressions on long-form or 1,000 views on Shorts.
174. **Refresh underperformers** — Swap a long-form thumbnail that is 30% below the channel's median CTR after 48 hours and 1,000 impressions.
175. **Thumbnail log** — Record each thumbnail's variables (emotion, gaze, colour, text yes/no, threat type) and its metrics in a shared spreadsheet.

## Ethics and Avoiding Misleading Clickbait

176. **Everything is in the video** — Depict only characters, objects and moments that actually appear in the episode.
177. **No fake interface** — Never add fake play buttons, notification badges, verified ticks or view counts.
178. **Fiction stays fiction** — Never frame an invented story as real news or label it "TRUE STORY" unless it is.
179. **Cartoon-level fear** — Keep scares stylised and non-graphic, and set the audience and Made for Kids setting accurately.
180. **Respect real places** — Portray real countries and cultures, such as Norway, without stereotype or mockery.
181. **No real likenesses** — Never use the faces or likenesses of real people or celebrities.
182. **Honest twist payoff** — Ensure the twist genuinely answers the question the thumbnail raises.
183. **Accurate nature facts** — In nature stories, never exaggerate a real creature's danger or abilities beyond the facts the episode presents.
184. **Platform disclosures** — Apply YouTube's altered or synthetic content disclosure and TikTok's AI-generated label whenever their rules require it.
185. **Photosensitivity safety** — Avoid more than three flashes per second in the opening seconds and any large saturated-red strobing.

## Final QC Checklist

186. **Dimensions verified** — Confirm 1080×1920 px for 9:16 and at least 1280×720 px for 16:9 before export.
187. **Safe-zone overlay** — Lay the Shorts, TikTok and 16:9 timestamp overlay templates over the image and confirm nothing essential is covered.
188. **Glance tests passed** — Confirm the image passed the 100 ms flash test with at least three people.
189. **Feed-size pass** — Confirm subject and emotion read at 180×320 px.
190. **Value pass** — Confirm the grayscale version still separates hero, object and threat.
191. **Expression pass** — Confirm enlarged eye discs, fear brows and a full-intensity mouth are present.
192. **Gaze pass** — Confirm pupils point either at the lens or at the threat, consistently.
193. **Threat pass** — Confirm the threat sits right behind the hero and is only partly revealed.
194. **Object pass** — Confirm the curiosity object is at least 15% of the frame, glowing and visibly strange.
195. **Text pass** — Confirm there is no text, or no more than three words that do not repeat the title.
196. **Frame-1 pass** — Confirm frame 1 is pixel-identical to the thumbnail and the cover is set on both platforms.
197. **Mode pass** — Confirm the image holds up on both #0F0F0F dark and #FFFFFF light backgrounds and under a colour-blind simulation.
198. **Compression pass** — Confirm no banding or smearing after a private test upload.
199. **Honesty pass** — Confirm every depicted element appears in the video and the twist is not spoiled.
200. **Archive** — Save the layered source and exports named like `S01E07_blank-library_thumb_v2_9x16.png` alongside the metrics log entry.
